# An offshore bank or digital-asset firm serving US residents: US law, US partners, tax regimes (as of 2026-11-24)

Scope note: this covers what changes, and what does not, if the operating entity is an offshore bank or offshore VASP rather than a US company on Dakota. It is research, not legal advice. Items marked **[counsel]** need a lawyer's decision. Quotes from eCFR and Cornell LII were read through a summarising fetch tool, so short quotes are exact but some paragraphs are paraphrased. eCFR.gov blocked automated fetches, so Cornell LII copies of the same CFR sections were used. Some facts come from the companion notes in this folder (`federal_and_dakota_model.md`, `us_state_licensing.md`, `stablecoin_law_and_charters.md`, `us_consumer_privacy_operational.md`); these are marked "(companion note)".

## 1. Can an offshore bank solicit deposits from, or open accounts for, US residents without US authorisation?

### Takeaway
No US licence or exemption lets an offshore bank with no US office market deposit accounts to US residents. Federal law makes it a crime for any person to engage "to any extent whatever" in the business of receiving deposits unless it is chartered, authorised and examined under US federal or state law (12 USC 378(a)(2)). Any US office that solicits business needs prior Federal Reserve approval. Even then, a representative office may not take deposits, and an office that takes deposits from US residents is a branch that needs a licence. US regulators have long treated offshore "banks" that solicit US depositors online as unauthorised banking. The only practical room is truly unsolicited relationships ("reverse solicitation"). I found no codified reverse-solicitation exemption in US banking law, and how far §378 reaches a bank that never sets foot in the US is a question for counsel. Adding any yield or investment feature brings in the SEC and state securities regulators. Adding derivatives brings in the CFTC.

### Cited Findings
**Federal banking statutes**
- 12 USC 378(a)(2) makes it unlawful "for any person, firm, corporation ... to engage, to any extent whatever with others than his or its officers, agents or employees, in the business of receiving deposits subject to check or to repayment upon presentation of a pass book, certificate of deposit, or other evidence of debt, or upon request of the depositor". The only exception is a person that is (A) "incorporated under, and authorized to engage in such business by, the laws of the United States or of any State, Territory, or District" and subject to examination there, (B) permitted by the US or a state to engage in the business and subject to its examination, or (C) a person that submits to examination by the state banking authority "where such business is carried on" and publishes condition reports. — [12 USC 378, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/378)
- Penalty under §378(b): whoever "shall willfully violate" the section can be "fined not more than $5,000 or imprisoned not more than five years, or both". Any officer, director, employee or agent "who knowingly participates" faces the same. — [12 USC 378, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/378)
- The Cornell text of §378 has no exception for deposits taken or booked outside the US. One search aggregator attributed to §378 a carve-out for "any deposit which is payable only at an office of a member bank located outside" the States. That language concerns member banks, not foreign banks, and does not appear in the Cornell text of §378. Treat it as misattributed. — [12 USC 378, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/378); contrast [govregs copy](https://www.govregs.com/uscode/expand/title12_chapter3_subchapterX_section378)
- International Banking Act definitions, 12 USC 3101:
  - "Agency": an office of a foreign bank in a State "at which deposits may not be accepted from citizens or residents of the United States".
  - "Branch": an office of a foreign bank in a State "at which deposits are received".
  - "Representative office": an office of a foreign bank in a State that is not a branch or agency.
  - "Foreign bank": "any company organized under the laws of a foreign country, a territory of the United States, Puerto Rico, Guam, American Samoa, or the Virgin Islands, which engages in the business of banking". A Puerto Rico bank is therefore a "foreign bank" for IBA purposes.
  — [12 USC 3101, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/3101)
- Regulation K: a representative office may solicit new business for the foreign bank but may not contract for any deposit or deposit-like liability. — [12 CFR Part 211 (2017 text), govinfo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CFR-2017-title12-vol2/xml/CFR-2017-title12-vol2-part211.xml)
- The Board must approve a representative office. It weighs the bank's home-country business and its consolidated supervision. Examples are a 2016 order (Swiss bank) and a February 2026 order (Honduran bank, Miami). — [Fed order 2016](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/orders20160624a1.pdf); [Fed order Feb 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/orders20260212a1.pdf)
- In approving one representative office (eBANK), the Board relied on the bank's commitment that the office would not solicit US deposits. — [Federal Reserve Bulletin, eBANK order](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubS/bulletin/2008/legal/q108/order8.htm)
- Board interpretations treat an office that takes deposits from US citizens or residents as a branch, not an agency. — [Board Interpretations of Regulation K](https://www.federalreserve.gov/frrs/regulations/board-interpretations-of-regulation-k.htm)
- In 1994 the Fed discouraged even US banks from soliciting US residents' deposits at their foreign branches unless the deposit serves an international purpose. — [SR 94-49](https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/srletters/1994/SR9449.HTM)

**How regulators treat offshore "banks" that solicit US residents**
- The OCC issues alerts on "entities engaged in unauthorized banking activities", offshore and domestic. Examples (search summaries; individual alert numbers not each confirmed):
  - A 1998 alert described an offshore shell bank "soliciting deposits on the Internet offering above market rate interest", whose deposits were not FDIC-insured and whose activities were "neither regulated nor otherwise supervised by any bank regulatory agency in the United States".
  - A 2007 alert said "the OCC has not authorized the referenced entity to operate as a bank or conduct banking business anywhere in the United States".
  — [OCC Alert 1998-7](https://occ.gov/news-issuances/alerts/1998/alert-1998-7.html); [OCC Alert 2007-32](https://occ.gov/news-issuances/alerts/2007/alert-2007-32.html)
- A 1997 OCC alert concerned a Barbados-registered entity whose advertising claimed "Class A bank" status and insured deposits. Regulators called this false and misleading, and the Idaho Department of Finance issued a cease-and-desist order. This shows state regulators act directly against offshore banks soliciting their residents. — [OCC Alert 1997-33](https://occ.gov/news-issuances/alerts/1997/alert-1997-33.html)
- Using "bank" in the name or marketing is separately restricted by state law. Chime (California and Illinois, 2021) and Dora Financial (California consent order, Nov 2024) had to stop calling themselves banks without disclaimers. New York restricts "bank" in names (Banking Law §132 per a DFS interpretation; the citation conflicts in commentary). — (companion note `us_state_licensing.md` §7); [TechCrunch on Chime](https://techcrunch.com/2021/05/05/chime-has-agreed-to-stop-using-the-word-bank-after-a-california-regulator-pushed-back/); [DFPI Dora consent order](https://dfpi.ca.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Consent-Order-Dora-Financial-LLC.pdf); [NYDFS interpretation](https://www.dfs.ny.gov/legal/interpret/lo070514.htm)
- FDIC Part 328 Subpart B (misrepresenting deposit insurance) applies to "any person". In 2022 the FDIC sent letters to crypto firms, including FTX US. — (companion note `us_consumer_privacy_operational.md`); [FDIC PR-60-2022](https://fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2022/pr22060.html)

**SEC / CFTC overlay for yield, investment or derivative products**
- SEC v. UBS (Feb 2009): UBS AG was charged with acting as an unregistered broker-dealer and investment adviser to **11,000–14,000 US "cross-border" clients** (1999–2008). It paid **$200 million** disgorgement and had to exit the business. The business was run by Switzerland-based client advisers who travelled to the US and used US jurisdictional means. A parallel DOJ matter added further disgorgement. — [SEC press release 2009-29](https://www.sec.gov/news/press/2009/2009-29.htm); [SEC Lit. Rel. 20905](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/litreleases/2009/lr20905.htm)
- SEC v. Nexo (Jan 19, 2023): the SEC found Nexo's "Earn Interest Product" was a security sold without registration or exemption. Nexo paid **$22.5M to the SEC and $22.5M to state regulators** and ceased the product "as to all U.S. investors". Bloomberg reported a different split, so confirm against the SEC order. — [SEC press release 2023-11](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2023-11); [The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/203983/nexo-pays-45-million-to-settle-sec-and-state-charges-for-failing-to-register-lending-product)
- CFTC Staff Advisory 25-27 (Aug 28, 2025) reaffirmed that non-US exchanges, including crypto exchanges, can give US participants direct access by registering as a **Foreign Board of Trade (FBOT)** under 17 CFR Part 48, instead of becoming a DCM. Registration is not automatic and requires comparable home-country regulation. Earlier offshore crypto derivatives venues that served US persons without registration faced CFTC actions; BitMEX's founders paid a $30M CFTC civil penalty (see Q3). — [Sidley](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2025/09/us-cftc-staff-issues-advisory-on-foreign-boards-of-trade-registration-clarifying-access-for-offshore); [MoFo](https://www.mofo.com/resources/insights/250909-cftc-staff-issue-advisory); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-08-28/cftc-says-offshore-crypto-exchanges-have-path-to-bringing-on-us-users)
- From **July 18, 2028**, the GENIUS Act makes it unlawful for a "digital asset service provider to offer or sell a payment stablecoin to a person in the United States" unless a permitted issuer issued it. The test is the customer's US location, not the provider's. — [GENIUS Act text](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ27/html/PLAW-119publ27.htm) (companion note `stablecoin_law_and_charters.md`)

### Inferences
- An offshore bank that **markets** to US residents (US-targeted ads, US app-store listing, US-dollar onboarding for US addresses, US events) is the fact pattern behind the OCC alerts, the state cease-and-desist orders, and the UBS case. "Reverse solicitation" is a defence only for truly unsolicited, customer-initiated relationships. A consumer app with US marketing cannot rely on it.
- A US marketing presence (founders in the US, a US office soliciting customers) moves the bank toward needing Federal Reserve approval for a representative office, which still cannot take deposits. Deposit-taking from US residents through a US office means a licensed branch, which is not realistic for a start-up offshore bank.
- Becoming an offshore **bank** does not remove the founders' US-person exposure. §378(b) reaches any "officer, director, employee, or agent ... who knowingly participates".
- A Puerto Rico licence is not an escape. PR entities are "foreign banks" under the IBA, but Puerto Rico is inside "the United States" for BSA purposes (see Q2 and Q3, Euro Pacific). **[counsel]**
- Any yield on balances (from Dakota RD rewards or other sources) offered by an offshore entity to US persons risks the Nexo/BlockFi outcome: an unregistered securities offering plus state securities actions.

### Gaps
- I found no court decision or agency interpretation on whether §378(a)(2) applies to a foreign bank with **no US presence** that accepts US residents' deposits purely online. **[counsel]**
- I found no law-firm analysis of state "doing business" tests for foreign banks soliciting online (for example, New York Banking Law provisions on foreign banking corporations). The UNC law review hit was old and general. — [UNC J. Int'l L.](https://scholarship.law.unc.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?httpsredir=1&article=1317&context=ncilj)
- No 2025–2026 OCC unauthorised-banking alerts were retrieved. Current practice is unverified.
- SEC Rule 15a-6 (exemptions for foreign broker-dealers, including unsolicited trades) and Regulation S were not fetched in this session.

## 2. FinCEN registration of foreign-located MSBs, and state money-transmitter licensing that follows the customer

### Takeaway
A non-bank offshore wallet or payments firm that does business "wholly or in substantial part within the United States" is an MSB under FinCEN's 2011 rule. It must register (Form 107) and name a US-resident agent for service of process. It also takes on the full BSA program, recordkeeping and reporting duties, even with no US physical presence. A true **foreign bank** is carved out of the MSB definition, but that does not license it to bank US residents (see Q1). State money-transmission and digital-asset laws attach to activity with the state's residents, so state licences are needed wherever the company is located. Under 18 USC 1960, operating without a required state licence is a federal crime. DOJ policy since April 2025 deprioritises non-wilful §1960 cases but has not changed the law.

### Cited Findings
- 31 CFR 1010.100(ff) defines an MSB as a person doing business "wholly or in substantial part within the United States" in a listed capacity (money transmitter, dealer in foreign exchange, and so on). — [31 CFR 1010.100, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)
- 1010.100(ff)(8): the term MSB "shall not include ... A bank or foreign bank". SEC- or CFTC-registered persons and comparable foreign financial agencies are also excluded. — [31 CFR 1010.100, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)
- 1010.100(u), "foreign bank": a bank organised under foreign law, or a foreign bank's office outside the US. The term "does not include an agent, agency, branch or office within the United States of a bank organized under foreign law". Those US offices are "banks" under 1010.100(d). — [31 CFR 1010.100, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)
- 1010.100(hhh) and (zz): "United States" includes the territories, starting with "The Commonwealth of Puerto Rico". — [31 CFR 1010.100, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.100)
- 31 CFR 1022.380(a)(1): "each money services business (whether or not licensed as a money services business by any State) must register with FinCEN". — [31 CFR 1022.380, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1022.380)
- 1022.380(a)(2): a foreign-located MSB "shall designate the name and address of a person who resides in the United States ... to be an agent to accept service of legal process with respect to compliance with this chapter". It must also identify a US location where records are kept. — [31 CFR 1022.380, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1022.380)
- 1022.380(e): "It is unlawful to do business without complying with 31 U.S.C. 5330 and this section". The civil penalty is "$5,000 for each violation", and "Each day a violation ... continues constitutes a separate violation". The section cross-refers to 18 USC 1960 for criminal penalties. — [31 CFR 1022.380, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1022.380); [31 USC 5330](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5330)
- FinCEN's July 18, 2011 release: the rule "requires foreign entities conducting these activities in the United States as MSBs to register". "An entity qualifies as an MSB based on its activity within the United States", and physical presence of US agents or offices is not determinative. — [FinCEN news release, July 18, 2011](https://fincen.gov/news/news-releases/fincen-clarifies-money-services-businesses-definitions-rule-includes-foreign)
- FinCEN Advisory FIN-2012-A001 set out the foreign-located MSB requirements. Registration and agent appointment were deferred until the revised Form 107 was available in 2012. — [FIN-2012-A001](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/FIN-2012-A001.pdf)
- FinCEN Advisory FIN-2019-A002 (May 2019): MSB registration, AML program, recordkeeping and reporting requirements "apply equally to domestic and foreign-located CVC money transmitters doing business in whole or substantial part within the United States, even if the foreign-located entity has no physical presence in the United States". — [FinCEN CVC Advisory FIN-2019-A002](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/advisory/2019-05-10/FinCEN%20Advisory%20CVC%20FINAL%20508.pdf); see also [FIN-2019-G001](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20Guidance%20CVC%20FINAL%20508.pdf)
- State licensing follows the customer:
  - OKX (Seychelles) pleaded guilty in part to operating an unlicensed money-transmitting business **under state law**. Manatt: under §1960, unlicensed transmission under state law "is a federal crime" where the state makes it a crime, and the Seychelles location did not put OKX outside US jurisdiction. The plea did not name a state; the activity could have occurred in any state where OKX had customers. — [Manatt, Mar 14, 2025](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/federal-prosecution-cautions-crypto-industry-on-state-money-transmitter-laws)
  - California DFAL (mandatory since July 1, 2026) covers exchanging, transferring or storing digital financial assets "with or on behalf of a California resident". A "resident" includes anyone physically present in California for more than 183 of the last 365 days. Non-exempt firms without a complete application by July 1, 2026 "must cease covered activities with California residents". — [Jones Day](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2026/02/registration-under-california-digital-financial-assets-law-begins-march-9-law-takes-effect-july-1); [Elliptic](https://www.elliptic.co/blog/california-dfal-july-2026-licensing-deadline) (companion note `us_state_licensing.md`)
- DOJ enforcement climate:
  - Deputy AG memo "Ending Regulation by Prosecution" (Apr 7, 2025): prosecutors should not charge "regulatory violations in cases involving digital assets", including §1960(b)(1)(A) and (B) and BSA violations, absent evidence of **wilful** violation. §1960(b)(1)(C) (funds known to be criminal proceeds) is not limited. — [Steptoe](https://www.steptoe.com/en/news-publications/blockchain-blog/deputy-attorney-general-memorandum-ending-regulation-by-prosecution.html); [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/doj-announces-shift-in-crypto-enforcement-and-end-of-regulation-by-prosecution)
  - In August 2025 DOJ said it would not bring new §1960(b)(1)(C) charges against developers of truly decentralised, non-custodial software. This statement is not binding. — [WilmerHale, Sept 10, 2025](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20250910-doj-signals-approach-to-digital-assets-what-it-means-for-developers-and-platforms) (companion note)

### Inferences
- **Offshore non-bank VASP or wallet with US users:** it is still an MSB, must register with FinCEN and appoint a US agent, needs a full BSA program, and needs state MTLs, DFAL or BitLicense for every state it serves, or it must rely on a licensed partner's licences. Going offshore removes none of the US licensing and adds a US-agent filing. Dakota's agent model (companion note) is the only way to avoid holding licences directly, and that model is built for US platforms.
- **Offshore licensed bank:** it falls outside the FinCEN MSB definition, so no Form 107. That helps only if it does not serve US residents, because serving them raises the §378 and state unauthorised-banking problems in Q1. Whether a foreign bank is exempt under each state's money-transmission and DFAL bank exemptions varies by state. Many exemptions cover only US-chartered or state-licensed institutions. **[counsel]**
- The Blanche memo lowers the chance of a **criminal** case for non-wilful licensing failures. It does not bind FinCEN (civil penalties), state regulators or future administrations. Knowingly serving US users from offshore while geo-blocking on paper would look wilful (see Q3).

### Gaps
- Whether the CSBS Money Transmission Modernization Act's "depository institution" exemption, and DFAL's bank exemption, cover a non-US-chartered bank was not verified. **[counsel]**
- FinCEN has not published a numeric threshold for "substantial part". I found no FinCEN ruling quantifying it.

## 3. Enforcement precedents: offshore crypto and payments firms serving US users without licences

### Takeaway
Since 2023 the pattern is consistent. Offshore exchanges that served US users while claiming to exclude them pleaded guilty to unlicensed money transmission or BSA program failures. Penalties ranged from about $100M to over $4B, plus monitors or consultants, US-market exits, and founder pleas. The deciding facts were weak KYC, VPN workarounds that staff knew of or encouraged, and US marketing. In 2025 the political climate softened: founders were pardoned, and DOJ narrowed crypto "regulatory" prosecutions. The corporate pleas stand, though, and the cases remain the template for how US authorities read "offshore". Euro Pacific (Puerto Rico) shows a different risk: an offshore-style bank failing on capital and compliance, with depositors stuck in a liquidation that was still running in 2025.

- The dividing line in every crypto case is **effective exclusion**. A geofence plus a self-attestation is not enough if KYC data (US address, SSN, US phone, US bank on file, US IP or device) shows US persons and the firm keeps serving them, or if staff coach users around the block.
- Euro Pacific is the closest analogue to "start an offshore bank". Its failure was prudential and supervisory (capital and consent-order compliance), made worse by multinational tax scrutiny. - Pardons (BitMEX founders) and the 2025 DOJ policy shift lower criminal-prosecution risk for founders today. Corporate penalties, FinCEN civil liability, state actions and partner off-boarding are not affected. Policy can also change again.

## 4. What US infrastructure partners require to onboard an offshore bank or VASP (KYB, correspondent rules, shell banks, EDD), and does Dakota onboard foreign FIs?

### Takeaway
US banks treat a foreign bank customer as a **correspondent account**. That brings mandatory due diligence under 31 CFR 1010.610, and **enhanced** due diligence whenever the foreign bank holds an "offshore banking license": a licence that bars it from banking with locals or in the local currency, which describes many small-jurisdiction licences. US banks are flatly barred from serving **foreign shell banks** (no staffed, inspectable physical office) and must recertify foreign bank customers every three years. Foreign non-bank VASPs and money transmitters are "foreign financial institutions" under the same rules. Dakota publicly onboards non-US businesses: EIN only for US firms, an address exception for Cayman entities, and EDD that asks for "country of regulation and type of license". Dakota publishes nothing on onboarding **banks** or other FIs as customers, and its catch-all "High risk businesses" exclusion defers to its partner banks (Lead Bank, BMO, Erebor). Whether Dakota would take an offshore bank has to be asked directly. **[ask Dakota]**

### Cited Findings
**Regulatory definitions (31 CFR 1010.605)**
- "Correspondent account" (banks): "any formal banking or business relationship established by a bank to provide regular services, dealings, and other financial transactions". — [31 CFR 1010.605, Cornell LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.605)
- "Covered financial institution" for 1010.610 means banks required to have AML programs, SEC-registered broker-dealers, CFTC-registered FCMs and IBs, and mutual funds. For 1010.630 it means insured banks, commercial banks and trust companies, private bankers, US branches and agencies of foreign banks, credit unions, savings associations, Edge corporations and broker-dealers. **MSBs are not on either list.** — [31 CFR 1010.605](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.605)
- "Foreign financial institution" includes "any person organized under foreign law" that is "engaged in the business of, and is readily identifiable as" a dealer in foreign exchange or a money transmitter, judged as if it were located in the US. An offshore VASP or wallet therefore counts as an FFI for US banks. — [31 CFR 1010.605](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.605)
- "Offshore banking license": "a license to conduct banking activities that prohibits the licensed entity from conducting banking activities with the citizens of, or in the local currency of, the jurisdiction that issued the license". — [31 CFR 1010.605](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.605)
- "Foreign shell bank": "a foreign bank without a physical presence in any country". "Physical presence" means a place of business that is maintained by the bank, is "located at a fixed address (other than solely an electronic address or a post-office box)" with full-time staff and operating records, and is "subject to inspection by the banking authority that licensed" the bank. — [31 CFR 1010.605](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.605)

**Due-diligence duties (1010.610, 1010.630)**
- 1010.610(a): covered FIs need "appropriate, specific, risk-based, and, where necessary, enhanced" policies for correspondent accounts of foreign FIs. They must assess the FI's business and markets, the account's purpose and the FI's AML record, and monitor on a risk basis. — [31 CFR 1010.610](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.610)
- 1010.610(c): **enhanced** due diligence is mandatory for a foreign bank operating under (1) an offshore banking licence, (2) a licence from a jurisdiction designated non-cooperative by an intergovernmental body with US concurrence, or (3) a licence from a jurisdiction designated under Section 311. — [31 CFR 1010.610](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.610)
- 1010.610(b), the EDD steps:
  - enhanced scrutiny of the account;
  - **nested accounts:** determine whether the foreign bank "maintains correspondent accounts for other foreign banks that use the foreign correspondent account", and if so assess and mitigate the risk, including identifying those banks as appropriate;
  - for non-public banks, identify "each owner ... and the nature and extent of each owner's ownership interest" (generally 10% or more).
  — [31 CFR 1010.610](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.610)
- 1010.610(d): where due diligence cannot be done, the institution must have procedures to refuse to open the account, suspend transactions, file a SAR, or close the account. — [31 CFR 1010.610](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.610)
- 1010.630(a)(1): covered FIs may not open or maintain a correspondent account for a foreign shell bank. They must take reasonable steps to ensure a foreign bank's account "is not being used by that foreign bank to indirectly provide banking services to a foreign shell bank". There is an exception for a "regulated affiliate". — [31 CFR 1010.630](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.630)
- 1010.630(a)(2) and (b): for non-public foreign banks, US records of owners and of a **US-resident agent for service of process** must be kept. The safe harbour is a certification or recertification "at least once every three years". — [31 CFR 1010.630](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.630)

**Dakota Platform, public materials (checked 2026-11-24)**

An operating address is "Required for all businesses except those incorporated in the Cayman Islands". - Coverage claims: homepage "Countries supported: 140+"; "Why Dakota" page "130+ jurisdictions". The API's country list is just ISO codes, and being on it does not mean a country is permitted. — [Dakota homepage](https://dakota.xyz/); [Why Dakota](https://dakota.xyz/why-dakota); [Get countries API](https://docs.dakota.xyz/api-reference/info/get-countries.md)
- Prohibited jurisdictions (35): Afghanistan, Algeria, Bangladesh, Belarus, Bhutan, Burundi, China, DR Congo, Cuba, Gaza Strip, Guinea-Bissau, Haiti, Iran, Iraq, Kenya, Kosovo, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Mozambique, Myanmar, Nepal, North Korea, Pakistan, Qatar, Russia, Somalia, South Sudan, Sudan, Syria, Ukraine, Venezuela, West Bank, Yemen, Zimbabwe. ### Inferences
- **What an offshore bank would face at a US bank or BaaS partner:**
  - Correspondent-account due diligence, with EDD by default if its licence is an "offshore banking license" as defined.
  - Ownership disclosure down to 10%.
  - Nested-account questions about its own downstream FI customers.
  - Proof of a staffed, inspectable physical office, so it is not a shell bank.
  - A US agent for service of process.
  - Triennial recertification.
  - "Licence-in-a-box" offshore banks run from a registered-agent address are likely to fail the shell-bank test outright.
- Dakota is an MSB, not a "covered financial institution", so 1010.610 and 1010.630 do not bind it directly. Its partner banks are bound, and an offshore bank's money would move through Lead Bank FBO accounts. The bank's policies will flow down as Dakota's "High risk businesses" exclusion. Expect Dakota to treat an offshore bank as a sub-client-style EDD case or decline it.
- Dakota's commercial-only Terms fit a **business** customer (an offshore FI) better than a consumer app. But a foreign FI using Dakota to serve its own retail customers is a nested, third-party arrangement that Dakota's onboarding flags for EDD.
- A **regulated** offshore VASP or EMI (for example, with a licence in a FATF-compliant financial centre) is probably easier for Dakota and US banks to onboard than an "offshore bank" whose licence matches the 1010.605 definition. The licence name ("bank") makes things harder, not easier.

## 5. Card networks: can an offshore entity issue Visa or Mastercard cards, directly or through a BIN sponsor, and issue to US residents?

### Takeaway
Visa's public Core Rules license each BIN "for use in a single country" and require the BIN to be used only "in a country in which the BIN Licensee ... is licensed". The exceptions are narrow: multinational commercial cards, certain prepaid, airline programmes, and EU passporting. In practice, cards for US residents must come from a US-licensed issuer, such as a US BIN-sponsor bank or the issuer Dakota uses. An offshore bank or programme manager cannot point its home-country BIN at US consumers. Visa also requires that "non-fiat currency funds must be converted to a fiat currency before deposit into a Cardholder account", and needs Visa approval for programmes whose accounts take funds converted from non-fiat. Every stablecoin card programme is therefore a Visa-approved fiat-settling programme. Offshore stablecoin card apps (for example, RedotPay) exclude US residents "due to global sanctions and card network restrictions".

### Cited Findings
- Visa Core Rule 1.2.1.1, "BIN and Acquiring Identifier Use and Jurisdiction": a BIN Licensee or its Sponsored Member "must use a BIN ... only: For programs and activity as agreed and licensed by Visa; As specified by Visa requirements ...; In a country in which the BIN Licensee ... is licensed". "A BIN or an Acquiring Identifier is licensed for use in a single country, except as specified for the following: Visa Multinational Program; Visa Prepaid program; International Airline Program; In the Europe Region: A Member that has passported its license to a Host Country". — [Visa Core Rules and Visa Product and Service Rules, public edition 13 April 2024, v1.2](https://bo.review.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/download/visa-rules-public.pdf)
- Visa Rule 4.12.5.1 limits prepaid cards distributed outside the country of issuance to cards funded by a multinational corporation or a government. For corporate programmes the issuer must "Obtain prior written approval from Visa for each Prepaid Card program enabled for distribution in a different country", "Not engage in any activity that amounts to active solicitation and issuance outside the countries in which it holds a Visa license", and "Ensure compliance with local laws and regulations in each country of distribution". — [Visa Rules (public), §4.12.5.1](https://bo.review.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/download/visa-rules-public.pdf)
- Visa Rule 1.4.4.6, "Cardholder Account Currency": "Any non-fiat currency funds must be converted to a fiat currency before deposit into a Cardholder account linked to a Visa Card. Conversion ... must be carried out in accordance with applicable laws and regulations in the country of issuance. The Issuer must receive Visa approval before implementing a Visa Program linked to a Cardholder account that accepts deposited funds that were previously converted from a non-fiat currency". — [Visa Rules (public)](https://bo.review.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/download/visa-rules-public.pdf)
- Visa Rule 1.1.1.3: each Member must comply with all laws, including on "banking, financial institutions, payment systems, foreign currency exchange, money transmission, anti-money laundering ... sanctions (such as those administered by the US Department of the Treasury's Office of Foreign Assets Control ...) ... consumer protection", for each country in which it operates. — [Visa Rules (public)](https://bo.review.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/download/visa-rules-public.pdf)
- "Visa Multinational Program" (glossary): lets a Visa **Commercial** Card issuer support multinational companies' spending "in countries where the Issuer may not hold a Visa license". This is not a consumer route. — [Visa Rules (public), Glossary](https://bo.review.visa.com/content/dam/VCOM/download/visa-rules-public.pdf)
- The Mastercard Rules (June 3, 2025 edition) contain §1.7 "Area of Use of the License" and participation restrictions (§1.10.2). Earlier editions had "Extending the Area of Use" provisions. The rule text itself could not be retrieved (HTTP 403). — [Mastercard Rules PDF](https://www.mastercard.com/content/dam/mccom/shared/business/support/rules-pdfs/mastercard-rules.pdf)
- RedotPay lists the **United States** among 43 "restricted regions" where residents cannot get virtual or physical cards and which cannot be billing addresses, "Due to global sanctions and card network restrictions". — [RedotPay Help Center, Card issuance restrictions](https://helpcenter.redotpay.com/en/articles/14254838-card-issuance-restrictions)
- Dakota's cards are "Visa debit cards that spend directly from a customer's Dakota wallet". Dakota must activate the client for cards, each customer accepts card terms on a Dakota-hosted page, and cards may be unavailable for a given customer type or **country**. — [Dakota Cards](https://docs.dakota.xyz/documentation/cards/index.md); [Dakota Cards activation](https://docs.dakota.xyz/documentation/cards/activation.md) (companion note)

### Inferences
- **US residents:** cards must come from a US-licensed issuer and BIN. An offshore bank would need a US BIN sponsor, and the sponsor's bank regulators would then treat the offshore bank as a high-risk programme manager or FFI (Q4). The current setup, with Dakota as the card programme and its US issuer, is already the simplest compliant route. Going offshore adds nothing for US cardholders.
- **Non-US customers:** an offshore entity can act as programme manager with BIN sponsors licensed in the cardholders' countries or regions. Each country needs its own BIN licence unless an exception (EU passporting, multinational or prepaid rules) applies. That is why global stablecoin card apps keep country exclusion lists.
- An offshore entity becoming a Visa or Mastercard **principal issuer** itself requires network membership, which in practice requires a regulated FI licence in each issuing country. Not verified in network rules. **[ask networks/BIN sponsor]**
- The public Visa rules are the April 2024 edition. Later editions may differ.

- Mastercard's Area-of-Use text and any Visa US-region rule on non-US issuers soliciting US residents were not retrieved.
- The countries in which Dakota's card programme is available to non-US cardholders were not found in public docs. **[ask Dakota]**
- Whether US consumer-protection laws (Reg E; Reg II/Durbin for large issuers) would apply to a non-US issuer issuing to US residents was not researched.

## 6. Tax and reporting regimes for an offshore financial institution, and US tax exposure for US founders

### Takeaway
An offshore bank, custodial wallet or e-money issuer is a "foreign financial institution" under FATCA. It must register with the IRS, get a GIIN, and identify and report US account holders, either directly to the IRS or through its government under an IGA. Otherwise it faces FATCA withholding. Nearly every common offshore centre has an IGA in force (Cayman, BVI, Bahamas, Barbados, Mauritius, Panama, St Vincent, St Lucia and others are Model 1; Bermuda and Switzerland are Model 2). Seychelles has only signed. Belize, Vanuatu, the Cook Islands and Comoros are not on Treasury's list. The same entity also enters the OECD CRS, which was amended ("CRS 2.0") from 1 January 2026 in most jurisdictions to cover e-money and CBDCs. If it holds crypto-assets (USDC) for customers, it is also a reporting crypto-asset service provider under the OECD CARF. Data collection began 1 January 2026 in about 47–52 jurisdictions, including the Cayman Islands, with first exchanges in 2027. The US is listed for 2029 and has not finalised CARF rules. US founders who own more than 50% are US shareholders of a CFC. They face Subpart F and the new NCTI regime (the renamed GILTI, effective for tax years beginning after 2025), Form 5471, and FBAR for signature authority over the company's foreign accounts.

- Under the final FATCA regulations, an FFI registers on the IRS FATCA registration website and gets a GIIN that appears on the IRS FFI list. FFIs in IGA jurisdictions may register and certify their IGA status to withholding agents. Treasury can remove a jurisdiction that does not bring its IGA into force, after which FFIs there can no longer rely on the IGA to be treated as compliant and exempt from withholding. — [Treasury FATCA page](https://home.treasury.gov/policy-issues/tax-policy/foreign-account-tax-compliance-act)

- Tax transparency pressure is real. The J5 (US plus four countries) investigated Euro Pacific for products that "facilitate ... tax evasion". — [IRS-CI J5 release](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-ci/j5-media-release-6-30-2022.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Reporting stack for an offshore stablecoin bank, wallet or EMI:**
  1. FATCA: register, get a GIIN, report US persons (Model 1 IGA: to the local authority; Model 2: directly to the IRS).
  2. CRS 2.0: report all non-local tax residents' depository, e-money and custodial accounts.
  3. CARF: report crypto-asset transactions and positions (for example USDC) for users resident in CARF jurisdictions, with self-certification of tax residence at onboarding.
  4. Local AML, travel rule and licensing duties.
  Tax-residence self-certification and TIN capture are needed at onboarding for every customer, in or out of the US.
- Building on a jurisdiction **without** an IGA (Belize, Vanuatu, Cook Islands, Comoros) means signing an FFI agreement directly with the IRS, and US correspondents treat it as higher risk. This compounds the "offshore banking license" EDD problem in Q4.
- **US founders with control:** an offshore company majority-owned by US persons is a CFC. Profits are taxed currently in the founders' hands either as Subpart F income (if passive or financial and §954(h) does not apply; a thin offshore bank may fail the "substantial activity" test) or as NCTI. Each founder files Form 5471 annually, plus FBAR for company accounts they can sign on. Offshore incorporation gives little deferral to US founders; the cost is compliance. **[US tax counsel]**

## 7. The common legitimate model: an offshore entity for non-US customers (geo-blocked, with US-person attestation) plus a US entity for US customers through licensed partners. How do comparable stablecoin neobanks do it?

### Takeaway
The model that holds up is a **hard split**. A US entity serves US persons through US-licensed partners (today's Dakota setup) or its own licences. A non-US entity, licensed where it actually operates, serves only non-US persons and screens out US persons using the KYC data itself, not just IP geofencing. RedotPay is the clearest current example: it bars US residents from its cards, and its separate US subsidiary has begun getting state MTLs (first licence Aug 2026, more than 20 pending, not yet transmitting). OKX relaunched a separate US business after its 2025 plea. The cautionary versions are Binance.com versus Binance.US and OKX before 2025: US users still reached the offshore entity through VPNs and staff help, and that drove the guilty pleas. The offshore side also has to respect other countries' cross-border rules; for example, the EU is tightening reverse solicitation for third-country banks under CRD VI from 2027.

### Cited Findings
- RedotPay: the US is a restricted region for card issuance ("Due to global sanctions and card network restrictions"), and US addresses cannot be billing addresses. — [RedotPay Help Center](https://helpcenter.redotpay.com/en/articles/14254838-card-issuance-restrictions)
- RedotPay's US subsidiary, **Red Dot Payment (US), LLC**, received its first US state money-transmitter licence (reported Aug 27, 2026), with applications pending in more than 20 states. It had "not conducted any money transmission" yet; reported volumes go through non-US regulated payment processors. — [The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/crypto-web3-and-cbdc/news/redotpay-obtains-its-first-us-money-transmitter-licence)
- OKX: the Seychelles entity (Aux Cayes FinTech) pleaded guilty in Feb 2025. A separate US business relaunched in April 2025 with a San Jose HQ and its own CEO. — [Fortune](https://fortune.com/crypto/2025/04/15/okx-relaunch-us-exchange-wallet-san-jose-california-new-ceo-roshan-robert); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-04-16/crypto-firm-okx-launches-us-exchange-after-settling-doj-charges)
- Why the split failed before:
  - Binance helped VIP customers get around IP blocking and told staff to encourage US users to hide their US ties. — [IRS-CI](https://www.irs.gov/node/128521)
  - OKX allegedly helped US customers use VPNs to bypass the US geofence and marketed at US events. — [Manatt](https://www.manatt.com/insights/newsletters/client-alert/federal-prosecution-cautions-crypto-industry-on-state-money-transmitter-laws)
- KAST (stablecoin card): sources conflict on US availability. ### Inferences
- **What "going offshore" changes for this product:**
  - *For US customers: nothing helpful.* FinCEN MSB status (unless a true bank), state MTL, DFAL and BitLicense, §378 and state banking-law exposure if it is a bank, SEC and state securities law for any yield, the GENIUS Act DASP rule from July 2028, card-network single-country BIN rules, FDIC misrepresentation and "bank"-name rules, and OFAC for US-person founders all still apply. It adds FATCA status, US-agent filings, correspondent EDD and CFC tax. US customers should stay on the US entity and Dakota (or later direct licences).
  - *For non-US retail customers: it can make sense,* if the offshore entity is properly licensed where it operates (staffed, inspectable office, so it is not a shell bank), excludes US persons by KYC data and not just IP, refuses VPN and US-nexus signals, and complies with each target country's cross-border rules (for example, EU MiCA, CRD VI and local reverse-solicitation limits).
  - *For businesses:* Dakota already onboards non-US businesses (EIN optional for non-US, Cayman address exception, 140+ countries claimed). A foreign business customer does not by itself require an offshore operating entity.
- **Geo-blocking and attestation controls the cases point to:**
  - US-person checks at onboarding: nationality, citizenship, US tax residence (W-9/W-8BEN-style self-certification, which FATCA needs anyway), residential address, phone, and ID document country.
  - Continuous signals: IP/ASN and VPN detection, device locale, US bank or card funding sources, US-routed fiat rails.
  - Written policy forbidding staff from helping customers get around the controls.
  - Periodic look-backs with off-boarding of US persons found.
  - No US marketing: app-store territory exclusions, no US events, no US-targeted ads.
- Using the word "bank" for an offshore entity that sends any marketing into the US multiplies the exposure (Q1). A non-bank licence (EMI or VASP) for the offshore arm is likely to be both easier to bank with in the US and less exposed. **[counsel]**
- Founders in the US running an offshore entity day-to-day weaken the claim that the entity is "foreign-located" and not doing business in the US. They also create US-person OFAC, §378(b) and §1960 accomplice exposure. **[counsel]**

### Gaps
- I did not find a primary source describing a stablecoin **neobank** (as opposed to an exchange or card app) with a documented dual US/offshore entity structure. Comparable examples were limited to RedotPay and OKX.
- KAST's primary terms were not checked, so its US eligibility is unresolved.
- No regulator guidance was found on what geo-blocking and attestation standard counts as "sufficient". The standard has been set by enforcement facts, not rules.
