# Federal licensing status of a consumer stablecoin app built on Dakota Platform, and what Dakota and partner banks require

Scope: US federal law only (state money-transmitter licensing is covered by another researcher). Current as of 2026-11-24. Research notes, not legal advice; items marked "counsel" need a lawyer's decision. Primary sources were read directly where possible (FinCEN guidance PDFs, eCFR, 31 U.S.C. 5330, Dakota docs/terms, Bridge developer agreement, 2024 joint statement, Evolve order). Secondary sources (law-firm alerts, press) are marked as such.

Facts about the build that the analysis relies on (from the brief): each user is a Dakota *individual customer* onboarded through Dakota-hosted KYC; each user has an EVM wallet on Base holding USDC; the app holds the ONLY signing key (one ES256 platform signer, policy approval threshold 1), so the app's server signs every send; deposits via per-user virtual ACH details (USD to USDC); withdrawals via Dakota off-ramp; **user-to-user transfers are off-chain book entries in the app's own database, with Dakota not involved**; planned Dakota cards and possibly RD with rewards.

## Q1. As built, is the app itself likely a money transmitter / MSB under federal law despite running "under Dakota's licenses"? Which design facts drive that, and which changes would move it out?

### Takeaway
As currently built, the app very likely is a money transmitter in its own right under 31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5) and FinCEN's 2019 CVC guidance. Two facts drive this: (1) it holds the only signing key, which gives it "total independent control" over user funds, making it a hosted-wallet provider whatever label Dakota uses; and (2) it runs its own ledger of user-to-user book transfers that Dakota never sees, so it accepts value from one user and transmits it to another. Dakota's licences do not cover activity Dakota does not perform, and none of the recognised exits currently applies: there is no agency agreement, the payment-processor exemption does not fit, and the user does not control the keys. The cleanest way out is to make transfers between users on-chain through Dakota and to give users control of the keys, or require a user co-signature, for both transactions and governance changes.

### Cited Findings

**The regulatory definition and exemptions**
- Money transmission services means "the acceptance of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency from one person and the transmission of currency, funds, or other value that substitutes for currency to another location or person by any means"; it also covers "any other person engaged in the transfer of funds." Status is "a matter of facts and circumstances." — [31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5), eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-A/section-1010.100)
- The six exclusions apply only to a person that *only* does the listed activity: (A) delivery, communication or network-access services used by a money transmitter; (B) payment processor facilitating purchase of goods/services or bill payment "through a clearance and settlement system by agreement with the creditor or seller"; (C) clearance/settlement intermediary solely between BSA-regulated institutions; (D) armored car; (E) provides prepaid access; (F) accepts/transmits funds "only integral to the sale of goods or the provision of services, other than money transmission services." — [31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(5)(ii)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-A/section-1010.100)

**FinCEN 2019 CVC guidance (FIN-2019-G001, May 9, 2019)**
- Labels do not decide the outcome: "The label, however, will not determine the regulatory application. Rather, this guidance applies to any business model that fits the same key facts and circumstances ... regardless of its label." — [FinCEN FIN-2019-G001, §1](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- A person is a money transmitter if it receives value "from one person and transmit[s] either the same or a different form of value to another person or location, by any means." It "may accept and transmit value in either order", meaning it can transmit first and accept the matching value later. It can operate "on an account basis", where it "may maintain an account for the transactor to store funds." — [FIN-2019-G001 §2](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- How intermediaries are treated "depends on four criteria: (a) who owns the value; (b) where the value is stored; (c) whether the owner interacts directly with the payment system where the CVC runs; and, (d) whether the person acting as intermediary has total independent control over the value." — [FIN-2019-G001 §4.2](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- Hosted wallet providers "are account-based money transmitters". The hallmarks are that "the value may be stored in a wallet or represented as an entry in the accounts of the host", "the owner interacts directly with the host, and not with the payment system", and "the host has total independent control over the value (although it is contractually obligated to access the value only on instructions from the owner)." — [FIN-2019-G001 §4.2.1](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- Unhosted wallets: "the owner interacts with the payment system directly and has total independent control over the value". — [FIN-2019-G001 §4.2.1](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- Multi-signature wallets: "If the multiple-signature wallet provider restricts its role to creating un-hosted wallets that require adding a second authorization key to the wallet owner's private key in order to validate and complete transactions, the provider is not a money transmitter because it does not accept and transmit value." But "if the person combines the services of a multiple-signature wallet provider and a hosted wallet provider, that person will then qualify as a money transmitter. Likewise, if the value is represented as an entry in the accounts of the provider, the owner does not interact with the payment system directly, or the provider maintains total independent control of the value, the provider will also qualify as a money transmitter, regardless of the label the person applies to itself or its activities." — [FIN-2019-G001 §4.2.2](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- Payment-processor exemption: there are four conditions, and all must be met. The person must "(a) facilitate the purchase of goods or services, or the payment of bills for goods or services (not just the money transmission itself); (b) operate through clearance and settlement systems that admit only BSA-regulated financial institutions; (c) provide its service pursuant to a formal agreement; and (d) enter a formal agreement with, at a minimum, the seller or creditor that provided the goods or services and also receives the funds." CVC processors "generally [are] unable to satisfy the second condition". The guidance cites FIN-2014-R012 (virtual currency payment system), FIN-2013-R002 (payable-through drafts) and FIN-2014-R009. — [FIN-2019-G001 §4.6 and fn. 66–67](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- Network-access exemption, as illustrated: a trading platform that only provides a forum, where "the parties themselves settle any matched transactions through an outside venue (either through individual wallets or other wallets not hosted by the trading platform)", is not a money transmitter. If it buys from the seller and sells to the buyer, it is (citing FIN-2014-R011). — [FIN-2019-G001 §5.1](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- A transmitter whose transactions are "transmittal[s] of funds" must comply with the Funds Transfer and Funds Travel Rules, and "must register with FinCEN within 180 days of starting to engage in money transmission." — [FIN-2019-G001 §2](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)

**The agent route**
- "A person that is a money services business solely because that person serves as an agent of another money services business ... is not required to register". A business acting "both on its own behalf and as an agent ... must register." — [31 CFR 1022.380(a)(3) (eCFR, via FinCEN 2026 notice)](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf); [eCFR 1022.380](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1022/subpart-D/section-1022.380)
- Agents are still MSBs and must have their own AML program: "The BSA requires all MSBs, both principals and their agents, to establish and maintain an effective written AML program ... both the principal and its agents remain liable ... neither the agent nor the principal can avoid liability ... by pointing to a contract assigning this responsibility to another party." — [FinCEN FIN-2016-G001 (Mar. 11, 2016)](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/shared/FIN-2016-G001.pdf); restated by FinCEN in [FR Doc. 2026-08363 (Apr. 30, 2026)](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf)

**What Dakota's public documents say about custody, agency and the regulated party**
- Dakota's docs: "True Non-Custodial Architecture ... We never have access to your private keys—not even temporarily, not even encrypted ... our infrastructure coordinates between your authorized signers, each maintaining their own keys." — [Dakota docs, Wallets](https://docs.dakota.xyz/documentation/wallets.md)

The same Terms say "due to the nature of our self-custodial wallet infrastructure, Dakota will never have access to the Authentication Credentials, and accordingly Dakota cannot and will not move any amounts on Customer's behalf." — [Dakota Terms §1.1](https://www.dakota.xyz/terms)
- Dakota's Terms §1.7: "Customer authorizes Dakota to act as Customer's agent and third-party sender to initiate, process, and execute payment orders, wire transfers, ACH transactions ... submitted by Customer through the Dakota Platform." — [Dakota Terms](https://www.dakota.xyz/terms). The public Terms never describe an intermediary platform or program manager, and never appoint one as Dakota's agent. "Authorized Users" are limited to the Customer's own "employees and/or contractors" (§1.2).
- Dakota's card docs assume the end customer holds the wallet key. To enable a wallet for cards, "Register the customer's key with `POST /signers`", and "the customer signs a card-settlement enablement". For the Cards terms, "Only the customer can accept. There is no API to accept on their behalf." — [Dakota docs, Wallet Card Enablement](https://docs.dakota.xyz/documentation/cards/wallet-enablement.md); [Activating Customers for Cards](https://docs.dakota.xyz/documentation/cards/activation.md)
- Dakota governance model: every non-transaction change (adding or removing signers, changing policies) "must be signed by the governance of the signer group that controls the resource". A group "is governed by its own members" unless another group is named, and the default quorum is 1. — [Dakota docs, Signing & Endorsed Requests](https://docs.dakota.xyz/documentation/signing-guide.md)
- Marketing claims: "KYC, KYB, sanctions screening and monitoring run on every customer and every payment, under Dakota's licenses and BSA/AML program". Roles: "Your team — Owns the customer relationship / Your customers / Your product / Support". "Dakota — Runs the compliance program / Licenses / Screening / Monitoring / Reviews". Also: "We agree the details together during onboarding." — [Dakota Compliance page](https://www.dakota.xyz/compliance). "Your flows run under Dakota's licenses, and regulators examine Dakota directly." — [Dakota Trust page](https://www.dakota.xyz/trust)

**Criminal exposure and DOJ posture**
- Failing to register is unlawful. The civil penalty is "$5,000 for each such violation", and "Each day a violation ... continues shall constitute a separate violation." — [31 U.S.C. 5330(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5330). 31 CFR 1022.380(e) notes that "18 U.S.C. 1960 provides a criminal penalty for failing to meet registration requirements." — [eCFR 1022.380](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1022/subpart-D/section-1022.380)
- In August 2025 DOJ said it would not bring new §1960(b)(1)(C) charges against developers of software that is "truly decentralized", "solely automates peer-to-peer transactions", and does not give any third party "custody or control over user assets." Commentators note the statement is not binding. The earlier Blanche memo addressed §1960(b)(1)(A)–(B). — [WilmerHale alert, Sept. 10, 2025](https://www.wilmerhale.com/en/insights/client-alerts/20250910-doj-signals-approach-to-digital-assets-what-it-means-for-developers-and-platforms); [DOJ, Galeotti remarks](https://www.justice.gov/opa/speech/acting-assistant-attorney-general-matthew-r-galeotti-delivers-remarks-american) (secondary summary; speech not re-read in full)

### Inferences

**Design facts and their likely federal effect**

| Design fact | Effect under FIN-2019-G001 / (ff)(5) | Change that would move it out |
|---|---|---|
| The app's server holds the **only** signer key (threshold 1). The app can move user USDC without the user. | This is the "total independent control" hallmark of a hosted wallet, even though the app is contractually bound to act only on user instructions (§4.2.1 says that does not matter). Dakota's "non-custodial" label describes Dakota's relationship with *its client*. It does not describe the app's relationship with the end user. Under §4.2.2 the provider qualifies "regardless of the label the person applies to itself". | Make the user's passkey or device key the required signer. Either it is the only transaction signer, or the user key and platform key form a 2-of-2 where the platform cannot act alone. **Also** require the user key in the signer group's governance quorum, so the app cannot rotate the user out or add a key of its own. Design recovery so the app cannot rebuild control on its own. |
| Transfers between users are **book entries in the app's own database**, and Dakota is not involved. | The app accepts value from user A and transmits it to user B (on an account basis, in "either order"). The value is "represented as an entry in the accounts of the provider". Dakota does not perform these transmissions, so Dakota's licences and BSA program cannot cover them. On-chain wallet balances also stop matching what users are owed, so the app becomes the ledger of record (the Synapse failure mode; see Q4). | Settle every transfer between users on-chain, wallet to wallet, through Dakota, signed by the sender's key. Dakota screens it and the chain plus Dakota hold the record. Keep no internal balances that differ from on-chain balances. |
| The app's server initiates deposits, withdrawals and sends on users' behalf. | If the user controlled the keys and instructed Dakota directly, the app could argue it only provides "delivery, communication, or network access services used by a money transmitter" ((ff)(5)(ii)(A)). Holding the keys defeats that argument, because the app itself authorizes each transmission. | Same as above: the user signs, and the app is only the interface. |
| No written agency or authorized-delegate agreement with Dakota Platform, Inc. Dakota's public Terms make Dakota the *customer's* agent and say nothing of the platform. | The 1022.380(a)(3) agent exemption is not available today. Even with an agency contract, the exemption covers only a person who is an MSB "solely" as an agent. Running its own P2P ledger would be acting "on its own behalf", so registration would still be required. | Sign a formal agency agreement with Dakota Platform, Inc. so that Dakota lists the app on its agent list and the app's AML program sits within Dakota's agent-monitoring program. Remove all own-account transmission (the internal ledger). Note that FIN-2016-G001 still requires the agent to have its own written AML program. |
| Payment-processor exemption | Does not fit. P2P, deposit and withdrawal flows are money transmission itself, not purchases from a seller (condition a). The internal ledger is not a clearance and settlement system limited to BSA-regulated institutions (condition b). There is no agreement with a seller or creditor (condition d). | At most this might matter for card spend at merchants, but Dakota and the card network, not the app, run that. |

- **Bottom line (counsel to confirm):** as built, the app is likely (1) a hosted-wallet money transmitter and (2) a P2P transmitter, so it would need its own FinCEN MSB registration within 180 days of starting, plus a full BSA program. State licences are a separate question, but operating without a required state licence is itself a federal crime under 18 U.S.C. 1960(b)(1)(A). "Running under Dakota's licenses" is accurate only for flows that Dakota itself performs and controls: onboarding KYC, ACH on-ramp, off-ramp, and on-chain transfers that Dakota screens.
- **The minimal re-architecture that plausibly takes the app out of federal MSB status:** (a) remove the internal P2P ledger and make every transfer an on-chain Dakota transfer; (b) give users the required key for both transactions and governance (passkeys as the sole signer, or a 2-of-2 where the platform key alone can do nothing); (c) never hold user funds in an app-controlled omnibus wallet or "float"; (d) make no promise to users that implies the app holds or moves their money. If the app wants to keep server-side signing for UX, the alternative is to give custody to a licensed custodian, that is, Dakota or Dakota's own key shard under the Dakota-Supported Wallet model, so that the licensed entity, not the app, has control. Whether Dakota offers that for consumer wallets is not public; ask Dakota.
- Dakota's own documents conflict on custody: the docs say Dakota "never" has key access, while the Terms describe a three-shard model with one shard used by Dakota. The team should ask Dakota which wallet model its platform clients' end-user wallets actually use. The answer changes who is the "host" under §4.2.1.
- Dakota's public Terms tell the *end user* that the wallet is "self-custodial" and that the Customer holds the credentials. In this build, the app holds them. That misalignment creates disclosure risk (UDAP/UDAAP; see Q5) and may breach Dakota's Terms, because Authorized Users must be the Customer's own employees or contractors. Raise this with Dakota and counsel.
- The planned passkey co-signer option is the right direction, but only if (i) the user's key is *required* (threshold met only with the user's signature), (ii) governance quorum also needs the user, and (iii) the app has no unilateral recovery path. Keeping a platform key that can sign alone "for convenience" keeps the app a hosted wallet.
- Card program, possible prepaid-access status: 31 CFR 1010.100(ff)(4) makes the participant with "principal oversight and control" over a prepaid program the "provider of prepaid access". Indicators are organizing the program, setting its terms, choosing participants, and controlling or directing parties to initiate, freeze or terminate access ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-A/section-1010.100)). Providers of prepaid access must register and identify each prepaid program ([1022.380(a)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1022/subpart-D/section-1022.380)). Dakota describes its cards as "Visa debit cards that spend directly from a customer's Dakota wallet", not prepaid ([Dakota Cards](https://docs.dakota.xyz/documentation/cards/index.md)). Counsel should confirm whether the card is "prepaid access" and who is designated provider, so the app does not become one by default.
- Certainty option: an MSB-status administrative ruling can be requested from FinCEN under 31 CFR 1010.711. Rulings take months, are fact-specific, and are published in redacted form. (Procedure from background knowledge; the eCFR section was not re-read this session.)

### Gaps
- No FinCEN administrative ruling was found that squarely addresses a "front end that holds the only key over wallets at a licensed infrastructure provider". The analysis applies §4.2.1 and §4.2.2 by analogy. This is a counsel call.
- Dakota's non-public platform client agreement or order form, which likely defines the client's role (agent, referral partner, or "program manager"), could not be reviewed. The public Terms do not describe a platform's role.
- Whether Dakota Platform, Inc. would appoint consumer-app clients as agents or authorized delegates is unknown. Its public materials frame the client as owning the customer relationship, not as an agent.
- The FinCEN MSB registrant search (msb.fincen.gov) is interactive and was not queried. Dakota Platform, Inc.'s FinCEN registration is taken from Dakota's own Terms and Trust page, not independently verified.
- The DOJ §1960 policy status in 2026 and the outcome of the Roman Storm appeal were not found. Neither helps a custodial design in any case.

## Q2. If the app is (or becomes) an MSB, or an agent of one: FinCEN registration and BSA/OFAC obligations, and the documents they require

### Takeaway
A principal MSB files FinCEN Form 107 within 180 days of being established, renews every two years by December 31 of the second year, re-registers after defined ownership or agent changes, and keeps records for 5 years. An agent does not register but must still have its own written, risk-based AML program with the four pillars. It must also meet the SAR, recordkeeping and Travel Rule duties that apply to its activity. OFAC compliance applies to everyone, on a strict-liability basis, with a 10-year record period since March 2025. FinCEN's April 2026 NPRM, which would reshape AML programs around risk assessment and "effectiveness", is pending.

### Cited Findings

**Registration under 31 U.S.C. 5330 and 31 CFR 1022.380**
- The registration must include: name and location of the business; name and address of each person who owns or controls it, is a director or officer, or "otherwise participates in the conduct of the affairs of the business"; the depository institution(s) where it keeps a transaction account; "An estimate of the volume of business in the coming year"; and other information Treasury requires. — [31 U.S.C. 5330(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5330)
- The registration is filed on FinCEN Form 107 (RMSB) through BSA E-Filing. The initial form is due "on or before the end of the 180-day period beginning on the day following the date the MSB is established". The initial period is two calendar years, the first renewal is due by December 31 of the second year, and renewals continue every two years after that. — [FinCEN notice, FR 2026-08363 (pub. Apr. 30, 2026)](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf); [31 CFR 1022.380(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1022/subpart-D/section-1022.380)
- Re-registration is due within 180 days after: a change in ownership that requires state re-licensing; a transfer of 10% of voting or equity interest (unless reported to the SEC); or an agent increase of 50% or more. A copy of the form must be kept for 5 years at a US location. — [FR 2026-08363](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf)
- Principals keep an agent list, revised each January 1 for the prior 12 months. It records each agent's name, address, phone, MSB activity types, the months with more than $100,000 in gross transactions, the depository institution holding the agent's transaction account, the first year as agent, and the number of sub-agents. It is not filed with Form 107 but must be available on request to FinCEN, law enforcement and the IRS. — [FR 2026-08363](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf)
- In April 2026 FinCEN renewed the Form 107 information collection *without change* (OMB 1506-0013; docket FINCEN-2026-0133). FinCEN counts about 24,856 active MSB principals and about 307,212 agents as of year-end 2025. — [FR 2026-08363](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf)
- MSBs are examined for BSA compliance by the IRS (31 CFR 1010.810(b)(8)). — [FR 2026-08363](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf)

**BSA program and reporting obligations (principals and agents)**
- MSBs must "(1) establish written AML programs ... (2) file Currency Transaction Reports (CTRs) and Suspicious Activity Reports (SARs); and (3) maintain certain records ... and certain transmittals of funds" (citing 31 CFR 1022.210; 1010.306–.314; 1022.320; 1010.410(e)–(f); 1010.430). — [FR 2026-08363](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf)
- Minimum AML program under 31 CFR 1022.210: policies, procedures and internal controls reasonably designed to assure BSA compliance; a designated person for day-to-day compliance (the BSA officer); education and training, including detecting suspicious transactions; and independent review. Agents must monitor and be monitored. Principals must identify agents' owners, evaluate agents' operations, review how agents implement policies, and run risk-based independent testing. — [FIN-2016-G001](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/shared/FIN-2016-G001.pdf)
- Travel Rule: "a transmittal of funds of $3,000 or more (or its equivalent in CVC) may trigger certain requirements on a money transmitter acting as either the financial institution for the transmittor or recipient, or as an intermediary financial institution", and the required information must be obtained or provided "before or at the time of the transmittal ... regardless of how a money transmitter sets up their system for clearing and settling transactions." — [FIN-2019-G001 §2](https://www.fincen.gov/system/files/2019-05/FinCEN%20CVC%20Guidance%20FINAL.pdf)
- SARs for MSBs fall under 31 CFR 1022.320. CTRs (cash over $10,000) fall under 31 CFR 1010.311 and following. — [FR 2026-08363 citations](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-08363.pdf). The MSB SAR threshold ($2,000) and 30-day filing deadline come from [31 CFR 1022.320](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1022/subpart-C/section-1022.320). These were taken from background knowledge of the regulation and not re-read this session.
- Pending reform: on April 10, 2026 FinCEN proposed a rule rewriting AML/CFT program requirements for all financial institutions, including MSBs (91 FR 18704; docket FINCEN-2026-0034). It supersedes and withdraws the July 3, 2024 NPRM, keeps the four pillars, adds a mandatory risk-assessment process that takes account of FinCEN's AML/CFT Priorities, and separates program *design* failures from *implementation* failures ("in all material respects"). Comments closed June 9, 2026. No final rule was found. — [Federal Register 2026-07033](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/10/2026-07033/anti-money-laundering-and-countering-the-financing-of-terrorism-programs); [DLA Piper, Apr. 21, 2026](https://www.dlapiper.com/en-de/insights/publications/2026/04/treasury-proposes-fundamental-reform-of-financial-institutions-aml-cft-program-requirements)

**OFAC**
- OFAC recordkeeping was extended from 5 to 10 years (31 CFR 501.601 and others), following the 21st Century Peace through Strength Act's 10-year statute of limitations. The interim final rule of September 13, 2024 was adopted as final on March 21, 2025. — [90 FR (Mar. 21, 2025), FR Doc. 2025-04864](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-03-21/html/2025-04864.htm)
- OFAC's expectations for a sanctions compliance program are its five-component "Framework for OFAC Compliance Commitments" (May 2019): management commitment, risk assessment, internal controls, testing/auditing, and training. It also published "Sanctions Compliance Guidance for the Virtual Currency Industry" (Oct. 2021), which covers geolocation/IP blocking, wallet screening and blockchain analytics. — [OFAC Framework](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/16331/download?inline); [OFAC VC guidance](https://ofac.treasury.gov/media/913571/download?inline). Both are from background knowledge; the documents were not re-fetched this session.
- Dakota already screens against OFAC's SDN list. Its Terms exclude persons in comprehensively sanctioned jurisdictions and on SDN or other restricted lists. — [Dakota Terms §1.3](https://www.dakota.xyz/terms)

### Inferences
- **Documents the app would need if it is an MSB principal** (and most also if it is an agent): (1) FinCEN Form 107 plus the volume estimate and a record copy, if a principal; (2) a board-approved written AML/CFT program, sized to the risk assessment; (3) an enterprise ML/TF and sanctions risk assessment covering products (USDC wallets, P2P, ACH on/off-ramp, cards, RD), customers (US consumers), channels (fully remote and mobile), and geographies; (4) BSA/AML officer appointment letter and CV; (5) KYC/CIP-equivalent and CDD/EDD procedures, which may rely on Dakota's hosted KYC under a written reliance or allocation agreement, though FIN-2016-G001 says liability cannot be contracted away; (6) transaction monitoring rules, including on-chain analytics for P2P and on-chain sends; (7) SAR decisioning and filing procedures with a confidentiality protocol and a SAR-sharing arrangement with Dakota; (8) Travel Rule procedures for transmittals of $3,000 or more; (9) recordkeeping and retention schedule (5 years BSA, 10 years OFAC); (10) OFAC sanctions policy covering screening, blocking/rejecting, and reporting within 10 business days; (11) training plan and logs; (12) independent testing plan, with the first test before or soon after launch; (13) law-enforcement request and 314(b) information-sharing procedures, if opted in; (14) an agent-oversight addendum, if acting as Dakota's agent.
- If the app re-architects so that it is neither an MSB nor an agent, it has no direct BSA obligations. In practice, though, Dakota and its banks will still require a written compliance program, a BSA contact, sanctions controls and SAR-referral procedures by contract (see Q3 and Q4). Expect to build most of this package regardless.

## Q4. What sponsor banks and BaaS/stablecoin infrastructure providers typically require from fintech program managers after Synapse: the due-diligence document package

### Takeaway
After Synapse, regulators focused bank-fintech oversight on the ledger and system of record, reconciliation, complaint handling, misleading marketing (especially FDIC-insurance claims), BSA/AML and OFAC, and contingency or wind-down plans. Consent orders such as Evolve's (Fed, June 2024) required new fintech partners to get prior regulatory approval. The deregulatory turn is real: in September 2026 the agencies proposed rescinding the July 2024 Joint Statement and replacing the 2023 TPRM guidance with lighter guidance that defers to bank judgment. But the 2023 guidance stays in effect until finalized, and banks and infrastructure providers still diligence program managers on the same core areas. A platform should expect to hand over a 15–20 item package.

### Cited Findings
- **July 25, 2024 Joint Statement (Fed/FDIC/OCC)** on bank arrangements with third parties delivering deposit products. It names as intermediaries the "intermediate platform providers, processors, middleware providers, aggregation layers, and/or program managers". Risks it lists: "Lack of access to records: A potential lack of sufficient access by a bank to the deposit and transaction system of record ... can impair the bank's ability to determine its deposit obligations"; third parties doing "monitoring and reporting suspicious activity, customer identification programs, customer due diligence, and sanctions compliance" while "the bank remains responsible"; Regulation E dispute timelines; "inadequate complaint administration and error resolution processes"; and "Potentially misleading statements and marketing ... particularly when they refer to FDIC deposit insurance". — [Joint Statement PDF](https://fdic.gov/joint-statement-third-party-deposit-products.pdf)
- Effective practices it describes: risk assessments specific to each arrangement; due diligence "of sufficient scope and depth"; contracts defining roles; ongoing monitoring; understanding the MIS "when the deposit and transaction system of record is managed through the third party"; "risk-based contingency plans" with contract terms allowing "transfer of the relevant accounts, data, or activities to another entity in the event of the third party's bankruptcy"; internal controls such as "dual control and separation of duties, payment data verification, and clear error processing and problem resolution procedures". It references the Interagency Guidance on Deposit Reconciliation Practices (May 18, 2016). — [Joint Statement PDF](https://fdic.gov/joint-statement-third-party-deposit-products.pdf)
- **Evolve Bank & Trust (Fed / Arkansas, June 14, 2024)**: the bank may not, without prior written approval, "establish any new fintech partners, subsidiaries, business lines, products, programs, services, or program managers related to [its Open Banking Division]". Approval requests must include "any proposed contract ... minutes approving the activity ... a description of the applicable measures to effectively manage the risk". The order also required "policies and procedures for fintech partners that address end customer user complaint identification, categorization, investigation, response, root cause and/or trend identification and analyses, remediation tracking, remediation validation, and periodic reporting to the Bank", and remediation of BSA/AML and OFAC deficiencies. — [Federal Reserve, Evolve order](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/enf20240614a1.pdf)
- **2023 Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Relationships: Risk Management** (88 FR 37920, June 9, 2023) sets the lifecycle (planning, due diligence, contract, ongoing monitoring, termination). It is the TPRM baseline cited by the Joint Statement. — [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2023/06/09/2023-12340/interagency-guidance-on-third-party-relationships-risk-management); cited in [Joint Statement fn. 10](https://fdic.gov/joint-statement-third-party-deposit-products.pdf). Its due-diligence factor list (strategies/goals, legal and regulatory compliance, financial condition, business experience, qualifications and backgrounds of key personnel, risk management, information security, MIS, operational resilience, incident reporting, physical security, HR, subcontractors, insurance, other contracts) is from background knowledge of the guidance and was not re-read this session.
- **Status change (Sept. 2026):** on September 11, 2026 the OCC, Fed, FDIC and NCUA proposed new "Interagency Guidance on Third-Party Risk Management". It would *replace* the 2023 guidance and *rescind* the July 2024 Joint Statement and the May 2024 community-bank TPRM guide. Due diligence becomes risk-scaled. The 17-item contract list is dropped ("no generally applicable expected terms"). Examiners must give "due consideration" to reasonable bank judgments. Comments were due about November 10–16, 2026, and "Until a final version is adopted, the 2023 Guidance remains in effect." Hunton notes that withdrawing the Joint Statement removes post-Synapse expectations "on account titling, ledgering, reconciliation, and access to customer-level records" with "no successor text", while "FDIC recordkeeping rules are unchanged." — [Hunton Andrews Kurth, Sept. 17, 2026](https://www.hunton.com/insights/legal/banking-agencies-propose-to-replace-the-2023-third-party-risk-management-guidance-and-to-rescind-the-bank-fintech-joint-statement) (secondary; the agency release was not read directly)
- **FDIC custodial-account recordkeeping NPRM** (Oct. 2, 2024, RIN 3064-AG07) would require banks holding custodial accounts with transactional features to keep records of each beneficial owner's balance and to reconcile. No final rule was found as of November 2026. — [89 FR, Oct. 2, 2024](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-10-02/html/2024-22565.htm)
- **FDIC "Conducting Due Diligence on Financial Technology Companies: A Guide for Community Banks"** (2021) organises fintech diligence around business experience and qualifications, financial condition, legal and regulatory compliance, risk management and controls, information security, and operational resilience. — [FDIC guide PDF](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2021/pr21075a.pdf) (six-topic list per search summary and background knowledge)
- BaaS providers' practice: initial diligence followed by "a full risk and compliance implementation that ultimately requires the Sponsor Bank's approval to launch with live customers". Requested items include BSA/AML/OFAC policies, account-closure and escheatment policies, and payment/fraud/dispute/ACH-return data. — [Synctera docs](https://docs.synctera.com/docs/due-diligence-and-matching-overview); [Synctera checklist](https://synctera.com/post/fintech-compliance-checklist) (search snippets)
- A running press list of 2024 BaaS bank consent orders exists. — [Banking Dive](https://www.bankingdive.com/news/a-running-list-of-baas-banks-hit-with-consent-orders-in-2024/729121/) (not opened)

### Inferences
**Due-diligence package a platform like this should prepare for Dakota and its partner bank(s).** Each item is tied to the guidance theme it answers.
1. **Corporate and ownership:** formation and governance documents, cap table, UBO (25% or more) and control-person IDs, background checks and résumés of founders and key staff, board/officer list, org chart. (Dakota KYB; TPRM "qualifications and backgrounds of key personnel".)
2. **Business plan and financial projections:** product description and flow-of-funds diagrams for deposit, P2P, withdrawal and card flows; target customers and states; volume, balance and revenue forecasts for 3 years; funding runway and source-of-funds evidence. (Dakota KYB and EDD; TPRM "financial condition"; banks' concentration and liquidity concerns in the Joint Statement.)
3. **Regulatory-status memo** from counsel: MSB/agent analysis (Q1), state licensing map, Regulation E and consumer-law position, stablecoin/GENIUS position (Q5). (Dakota EDD "Regulated / unregulated justification".)
4. **Compliance management system (CMS):** board oversight, compliance officer, policies inventory, change management, consumer-law compliance (UDAAP, E-Sign, Regulation E if applicable, GLBA privacy notice). (Joint Statement consumer-protection section.)
5. **BSA/AML program:** written policy, ML/TF risk assessment, CDD/EDD reliance on Dakota KYC, transaction monitoring (including on-chain analytics), SAR referral to Dakota, training, and an independent-testing plan. (FIN-2016-G001; Joint Statement "third parties performing compliance functions".)
6. **OFAC sanctions policy:** screening (people and wallets), IP/geolocation blocking of prohibited jurisdictions (Dakota's list), escalation, 10-year retention.
7. **Complaint management:** intake, categorisation, investigation, root-cause and trend analysis, remediation tracking, and periodic reporting to Dakota/bank. This mirrors the Evolve order's list nearly item for item.
8. **Marketing and disclosure review procedure:** pre-approval workflow with Dakota (and the bank), a ban on "bank"/FDIC claims, and a disclosure library (Q5). (Joint Statement "misleading statements and marketing".)
9. **Ledger and reconciliation:** identify the system of record; run daily three-way reconciliation of the app's records against Dakota's records and on-chain balances; set breaks/exception handling and give Dakota/bank access to customer-level records. (Joint Statement "Lack of access to records"; 2016 Deposit Reconciliation guidance; FDIC 2024 NPRM.) This is the strongest practical reason to drop the internal P2P ledger.
10. **Information security program:** written infosec policy, key-management policy (critical given the server-held signer key: HSM/KMS use, access control, dual control), vendor pen test, vulnerability management, incident response with notification timelines, privacy/GLBA safeguards, and SOC 2 Type I/II or a roadmap toward one. (TPRM "information security"; Joint Statement "dual control and separation of duties".)
11. **Business continuity and disaster recovery, plus a wind-down plan:** how users keep access to funds if the app fails. With user-held keys, users could withdraw directly through Dakota; with app-held keys they could not. (Joint Statement "contingency plans ... business failure".)
12. **Vendor and subcontractor management:** list of critical vendors (cloud, KYC, analytics, card processor), their SOC reports and contracts. (TPRM "reliance on subcontractors".)
13. **Fraud and disputes:** account-takeover controls, card dispute handling via Dakota's API, ACH return handling, and loss-allocation policy.
14. **Insurance:** cyber/technology E&O, crime/fidelity and D&O, with limits. (TPRM "insurance coverage"; amounts are commercial terms.)
15. **Customer-facing agreements:** app Terms and Privacy Policy aligned with Dakota's and the bank's agreements, E-Sign consent, fee schedule, and state complaint disclosures.
16. **Records and audit rights:** record-retention schedule and acceptance of audit/examination access for Dakota, the bank and regulators. (Bridge clause (l); TPRM contract terms.)
- The proposed September 2026 guidance may lighten *banks'* formal expectations, but banks under consent orders and a stablecoin provider applying for an OCC charter are unlikely to relax diligence in the near term. Plan for the full package. (Judgment, not sourced.)

## Q5. How comparable stablecoin neobanks and wallets on infrastructure providers disclose who is licensed, plus related federal stablecoin points (RD rewards)

### Takeaway
The prevailing pattern has four parts: (1) "X is a financial technology company, not a bank [or MSB]"; (2) name the licensed entity and its NMLS ID that provides the regulated service, often per region; (3) name the card issuer and network licence; (4) a clear not-FDIC-insured / not-legal-tender warning. Infrastructure providers such as Bridge contractually *require* platforms to show that the regulated services come from the provider, to pre-clear marketing, to forward complaints, and not to hold user keys or funds. It must keep "accurate and complete records of all User interactions". And it must "**not ... hold or control any crypto-assets or funds on behalf of Users ... or hold or control any private cryptographic keys that provide access to crypto-assets or funds held on behalf of Users.**" — [Bridge Developer Agreement](https://bridge.xyz/legal/developer-agreement)
- §2.5: the developer handles user support, disputes and recordkeeping through the API, and "will promptly report all Complaints ... to Bridge". Bridge "may communicate directly with Users" where law or regulators require. — [Bridge Developer Agreement](https://bridge.xyz/legal/developer-agreement)
- Bridge regulated entities "will have a contractual relationship with each User that is independent from Developer's contractual relationship with its Users". Bridge's prohibited list includes "money services (provided by Users to third parties), money transmission services (provided by Users to third parties)". — [Bridge Developer Agreement](https://bridge.xyz/legal/developer-agreement)

**Federal stablecoin developments relevant to the RD-with-rewards plan**
- The GENIUS Act (enacted July 18, 2025) takes effect on the earlier of January 18, 2027 or 120 days after final regulations. The OCC finalized its implementing rule on October 21, 2026, with effect from January 18, 2027 (OCC Bulletins 2026-50 and 2026-51). FinCEN and OFAC proposed separate AML and sanctions rules for issuers in April 2026. — [OCC News Release 2026-92](https://occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2026/nr-occ-2026-92.html); [The Block, Oct. 21, 2026](https://theblock.co/news/regulation/2026-10-21-occ-final-rule-implementing-trump-signed-genius-act-421470) (per search summaries; release not read in full)
- The OCC final rule bars issuers from paying interest or yield "solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention" of a payment stablecoin, to any "payment stablecoin holder" (the beneficial owner). The preamble gives as violations an agent paying yield "on the issuer's behalf" and a white-labeled partner receiving payments "likely to be made on behalf of" the issuer. The proposed rebuttable presumption was dropped with no safe harbor, and the OCC will "take action and provide guidance" on evasion. Possibly permissible: payments for bona fide services, transaction-based payments, and payments wholly for other purposes. — [Hogan Lovells Cadwalader, Oct. 29, 2026](https://www.hlc.com/en/publications/occ-finalizes-its-genius-act-rule-part-1-permitted-activities-and-prohibition-on-yield)
- Market-structure (CLARITY Act) yield compromise: Senate text released May 1, 2026 bars rewards "economically or functionally equivalent to interest on a bank deposit" on passive balances while allowing activity-based rewards. As of the latest sources found (May–Aug 2026) the bill was not law. — [Baker McKenzie, May 5, 2026](https://blockchain.bakermckenzie.com/2026/05/05/the-clarity-acts-yield-compromise-what-the-senate-actually-agreed-to-and-why-it-matters/); [The Block, Apr. 16, 2026](https://www.theblock.co/news/regulation/2026-04-16-clarity-act-stablecoin-yield-delayed-397838)
- Dakota markets RD as "GENIUS-ready", backed by Treasury money-market funds, with "Rewards on balances ... that you can keep or pass on to your customers." — [Dakota Issuance](https://www.dakota.xyz/issuance)

### Inferences
- **Possible disclosure wording for counsel to adapt.** It is accurate *only after* the re-architecture in Q1:
  "[Brand] is a financial technology company, not a bank or a licensed money transmitter. Account, wallet, and money transmission services are provided by Dakota Platform, Inc. (NMLS #2727274), a licensed money transmitter in the states listed at [licenses link]; see NMLS Consumer Access. [Card disclosure: issuer/network per Dakota's card program.] Your balance is held as USDC, a stablecoin issued by Circle; it is not a bank deposit, is not legal tender, and is not insured by the FDIC or SIPC. [State-specific complaint and regulator text from Dakota's Licenses page.]"
  Do not say "you custody your funds" or "non-custodial" while the app's server holds the only key. That would be a deceptive statement, risking UDAP/UDAAP under FTC Act §5 and CFPA §1031. Plasma can say "You do" because its users control the keys. Do not mention FDIC at all except to disclaim it (Joint Statement risk theme).
- Dakota's public materials have no Bridge-style platform clause. Bridge's (m) clause shows that a major peer infrastructure provider treats a platform holding user keys or funds as **outside** the licensed provider's umbrella, and so as an unlicensed-transmitter risk. Dakota's private agreement may contain the same; ask.
- **RD rewards:** if the app moves user balances into RD and passes on Dakota-paid "rewards", the payment could be seen as yield paid on the issuer's behalf through a partner. That is the fact pattern the OCC preamble flags, once Dakota or its issuing affiliate is a permitted payment stablecoin issuer subject to the rule from January 18, 2027. Activity-based rewards (for example, card-spend rewards) are the safer design. Counsel should decide before any "earn on your balance" marketing. Separately, an app that holds RD for users in its own name would add custody and broker questions. Keep RD in user-controlled wallets.
- Track three changes before launch: (1) final FinCEN AML program rule; (2) final Sept. 2026 TPRM guidance and withdrawal of the Joint Statement; (3) CLARITY Act enactment and its yield and "non-controlling developer" provisions, if any.
